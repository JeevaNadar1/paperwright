# India layer

What changes when the parties, the site or the governing law are Indian. These
are structural rules that decide whether a document is admissible and how money
actually moves, not drafting preferences.

Rates, thresholds and state schedules change with every Finance Act and with
state amendments. Treat every figure here as a prompt to verify, not as a
current number. Flag the verification in the output rather than asserting the
rate.

## 1. Stamp duty

Stamp duty is a state subject. The rate depends on the instrument type, the
state of execution, and sometimes the state where the property sits or where the
document is first received.

1. An under-stamped instrument is generally inadmissible in evidence until the
   duty and a penalty are paid. The contract is not void, but it cannot easily
   be used, which for a dispute document is the same thing.
2. Duty attaches to the instrument, not the transaction. Two copies can attract
   duty twice unless one is stamped as a counterpart at the lower counterpart
   rate, where the state schedule provides for it.
3. Common instrument categories: agreement, agreement relating to deposit of
   title deeds, lease, leave and licence, indemnity bond, power of attorney,
   conveyance, and share transfer. Each has a separate schedule entry.
4. Where an agreement is executed in one state and relied upon in another, the
   difference in duty may become payable in the second state.
5. In the document, state the place of execution expressly. It determines the
   applicable schedule and is routinely left out.

**In the output:** name the instrument category, name the state, and say that
the applicable schedule and current rate need confirmation before execution.
Do not state a rate.

## 2. Registration

1. A lease of immovable property for a term of one year or more generally
   requires registration. The common practice of an eleven-month leave and
   licence exists precisely to sit below that line.
2. Leave and licence agreements for residential and commercial premises are
   separately subject to registration requirements in several states,
   Maharashtra among them, regardless of the eleven-month framing.
3. An unregistered document that requires registration is generally not
   admissible to prove the terms of the transaction, with narrow exceptions.
4. Renewal options that take the aggregate term past the threshold can pull the
   whole arrangement into registration.

## 3. Execution, e-stamping and electronic signature

1. Electronic signatures are recognised under the Information Technology Act,
   but the recognised forms are specific: a digital signature certificate issued
   by a licensed certifying authority, or an Aadhaar-based eKYC e-signature.
   A pasted image of a signature is not one of them.
2. Certain instruments are excluded from electronic execution, including
   negotiable instruments, powers of attorney, trusts, wills, and contracts for
   the sale or conveyance of immovable property. Check before recommending an
   electronic close on anything touching property.
3. E-stamping is available in most states and is the cleaner route; the
   certificate carries the instrument reference.
4. Counterparts clause: state that the agreement may be executed in
   counterparts and that scanned or electronic copies have the same effect,
   where the instrument permits it.

## 4. GST on contract value

1. Whether rates are stated inclusive or exclusive of GST is the single largest
   avoidable dispute in Indian commercial documents. State it in the fee clause
   itself, not only in a schedule.
2. Place of supply determines whether the supply is intra-state or inter-state,
   which determines whether CGST and SGST or IGST applies. For services tied to
   immovable property, place of supply generally follows the property.
3. Reverse charge applies to certain categories and to supplies from unregistered
   suppliers in defined cases. Where relevant, state who discharges the liability.
4. Input tax credit depends on the invoice appearing correctly against the
   recipient's GSTIN. Make correct invoicing with GSTIN, HSN or SAC and place of
   supply a contractual obligation, with a make-good if credit is denied because
   of the supplier's non-compliance.
5. On a multi-year contract, include a change-in-law clause so a rate change is
   not absorbed silently by one side.
6. Both parties' GSTIN and registered address belong in the parties clause or a
   schedule.

## 5. TDS by payment type

Withholding is the counterparty's obligation, but it lands on your cash flow, so
the document should address the certificate and the timeline.

| Payment type | Common section |
|---|---|
| Contractual work, including construction and fabrication | 194C |
| Professional or technical services, royalty, non-compete fees | 194J |
| Rent on land, building, plant or equipment | 194I |
| Commission or brokerage | 194H |
| Purchase of goods above the threshold | 194Q |
| Payments to non-residents | 195 |

1. Rates, thresholds and the distinction between individual and entity payees
   change. Verify before stating a number in a document.
2. Where a lower or nil deduction certificate exists, reference it in the
   payment clause.
3. Contractual ask: the deductor furnishes the TDS certificate within a stated
   period, and credits the correct PAN. Non-issuance is a common cause of
   reconciliation failure at year-end.
4. For cross-border payments, the treaty position, the tax residency
   certificate and Form 10F drive the rate. Make furnishing them an obligation
   with a deadline.

## 6. MSMED payment timelines

1. Where the supplier is a registered micro or small enterprise under the MSMED
   Act, payment is generally due within 45 days of acceptance or deemed
   acceptance, and a contrary contractual term does not override the statutory
   position.
2. Delayed payment attracts compound interest at a statutory multiple of the
   RBI-notified rate, which is materially higher than commercial interest.
3. Buyers face disallowance consequences under income-tax provisions for amounts
   outstanding to such suppliers beyond the statutory period.
4. Practical drafting: require the counterparty to declare its MSME registration
   status and Udyam number in the agreement, and to notify any change. Both
   sides need to know, for opposite reasons.

## 7. Contract Act limits on standard clauses

| Clause | Indian position |
|---|---|
| Post-employment non-compete | Generally void under section 27 as a restraint of trade, with a narrow goodwill-sale exception. Restraints operating during employment are treated differently. |
| Non-solicitation of employees and customers | More defensible than a non-compete, but still read narrowly. Keep the duration short and the scope specific. |
| Clauses shortening the limitation period or barring remedies | Constrained by section 28. Arbitration clauses are carved out. |
| Liquidated damages | Under section 74, the stated sum is a ceiling; the claimant recovers reasonable compensation and may need to show loss, though not always the precise quantum. A penalty framed as deterrence is not recovered as such. |
| Indemnity and guarantee | Distinct concepts under sections 124 and 126. Drafting one and calling it the other creates avoidable argument. |
| Specific performance | Since the 2018 amendment to the Specific Relief Act, specific performance is the general rule rather than a discretionary exception for contracts within its scope. |

Do not copy a US-style non-compete into an Indian employment document and assume
it operates. Draft confidentiality, IP assignment, garden leave and
non-solicitation instead, which survive.

## 8. Arbitration

1. State the **seat** expressly. The seat fixes the supervisory court; naming
   only a venue generates a preliminary dispute.
2. Specify the number of arbitrators. A default panel of three on a small
   contract makes arbitration uneconomic. Use a sole arbitrator below a stated
   value threshold.
3. Specify the language, the institutional rules if any, and the appointment
   mechanism if the parties fail to agree.
4. Interim relief from a court remains available notwithstanding the arbitration
   clause; do not draft it away accidentally.
5. For a cross-border contract, decide deliberately whether the arbitration is
   India-seated, because that determines which part of the Arbitration and
   Conciliation Act applies.

## 9. Employment-specific points

1. Statutory contributions follow eligibility thresholds and headcount, not the
   contract's silence. Provident fund, ESI, gratuity and professional tax all sit
   outside the document.
2. Shops and establishments legislation is state-level and governs hours, leave
   and termination notice. A contract cannot fall below its floor.
3. Gratuity generally becomes payable after five years of continuous service.
   Offer letters usually mention it; few explain the eligibility condition.
4. Notice-period buyout should state the calculation basis, usually the fixed
   component rather than the full cost to company.
5. Contractor misclassification exposure is real where the arrangement has fixed
   hours, exclusivity, company equipment and a reporting line. Structure matches
   substance, or the label does not survive.

## 10. Property and leasing points

1. State the area basis expressly: carpet, built-up or super built-up, with the
   measurement standard. Disagreement here is the most common leasing dispute.
2. Security deposit clauses need an interest position, a refund timeline, and a
   closed list of permitted deductions.
3. Property tax, common area maintenance and major structural repair allocation
   belong in the document, not in an assumption.
4. GST applies to commercial rent; residential letting is treated differently.
   State the position in the rent clause.
5. Lock-in periods bind both sides only if drafted to. A lock-in that binds the
   tenant alone is a common and negotiable asymmetry.
6. For a fit-out, state the reinstatement obligation at exit and whether the
   landlord may elect to retain the improvements.

## 11. What to put in the document

1. Place of execution.
2. Both parties' PAN, GSTIN, CIN or registration number, and registered address.
3. MSME status declaration and Udyam number, where applicable.
4. Fee clause stating inclusive or exclusive of GST, with the applicable rate to
   be charged as in force.
5. Withholding clause with the certificate obligation and timeline.
6. Change-in-law clause on any contract running beyond one financial year.
7. Stamping responsibility: who bears the duty, and who arranges it.
8. Registration responsibility and cost sharing, where registration applies.
9. Counterparts and electronic execution clause, where the instrument permits.
10. Seat, language and arbitrator count in the dispute clause.
